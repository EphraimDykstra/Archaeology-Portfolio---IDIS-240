Foundations

What Is Archaeology?

The study of the human past through physical remains. Not just digging — a rigorous blend of science, humanities, and interpretation that recovers human stories writing alone can't tell.

🏺

Archaeology

The study of the Human Past. Starts with evidence, builds a claim. Examines material culture — what people left behind — to construct honest, evidence-based histories of the past. It is both a science and a humanity, operating across every period of human existence.


Key insight from Day 2: "For about 97% of human existence, archaeology is the only way to recover the human story — writing hadn't been invented yet."

🚫

Pseudo-archaeology

Starts with an idea, cherry-picks evidence to defend it. Examples: ancient astronaut theories, Atlantis claims, and racially motivated alternative histories. Pseudo-archaeology undermines real heritage by promoting myths over evidence.


The increasing ignorance framing: aliens built the pyramids; Atlantis explains human development. Each claim a failure of evidence-based reasoning.

Processual / "New" Archaeology

Science-First Approach

Associated with Lewis Binford (1960s–70s). Argues archaeology can discover universal laws of human behavior through scientific method. Culture Process is knowable and testable. Sometimes criticized for over-scientizing human behavior — treating people like variables rather than agents.

vs.
Post-Processual Archaeology

Interpretive Approach

Associated with Ian Hodder, Shanks & Tilley (1980s). Emphasizes subjectivity, symbolism, and plural interpretations. "The data doesn't say anything — we say things using data." Recognizes that archaeologists bring their own perspectives to interpretation. Archaeology is not just a science.

Disciplinary History

The Story of Archaeology

From ancient kings collecting artifacts to satellite imagery and LiDAR — archaeology evolved through major ideas, methods, and personalities.

c. 550 BCE
Nabonidus
Last king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. Ordered excavation of ancient temples in Iraq and Turkey, restored them, and displayed artifacts in a museum. Often called the Father of Archaeology.
c. 320 CE
Helena Augusta
Mother of Constantine the Great. Traveled the Holy Land identifying pilgrimage sites and seeking Christian relics. Patron Saint of Archaeologists. Theory: Constantine's interest in Christianity was partly shaped by his mother's faith.
16th–17th c.
Cabinets of Curiosities & British Antiquarians
Rooms filled with artifacts, relics, and rarities owned by wealthy men — early proto-museums. Chorographers like Sir Robert Sibbald (who first used the word "Archaeologie") studied regional culture, geography, and history, developing early survey methods.
1748
Pompeii Excavations Begin
The first intentional excavation conducted to seek historical information — not just to loot. Marks the transition from treasure-hunting to structured inquiry into the past.
1784
Thomas Jefferson — First Scientific Excavation
On his Monticello estate, Jefferson noticed unusual mounds. He excavated them methodically, observing that layers were different — recognizing stratigraphy for the first time. The mounds proved to be Native American burial sites. This is the first excavation to use what became standard archaeological practice.
1836
C.J. Thomsen — The Three Age System
Danish archaeologist who organized Copenhagen museum collections into Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages based on material culture. Created the foundational chronological framework for prehistoric archaeology — still used today.
1859
Darwin & Deep Time
On the Origin of Species established evolution by natural selection. Combined with geological evidence of an ancient Earth, this gave archaeology the deep time framework it required: humans have existed far longer than any written record suggests.
20th Century
Wheeler & Kenyon — Grid Excavation
Mortimer Wheeler developed the box-grid method (retaining earthen balks for stratigraphic profiles). Kathleen Kenyon refined it at Jericho — and in the process pushed the dates of occupation back several centuries, frustrating those who had used Jericho as biblical proof. Her evidence mattered more than the conclusion people hoped for.
Present
Digital & Remote Sensing Revolution
LiDAR at Caracol (Belize) revealed an entire hidden city through jungle canopy. GPR at Forum Novum mapped buried Roman streets without digging a single trench. GIS integrates spatial data across layers. The way we find and map sites has been transformed.
The Material Past

The Archaeological Record

Everything left behind by past human activity. The four major categories of evidence — and why the single most important concept in all of archaeology is context.

🏺

Artifacts

Human-made or modified objects that are portable without machinery. Pottery, tools, coins, weapons. The most common and most studied category. Ceramics serve as the "chronological backbone" of archaeology — abundant, indestructible, and rapidly changing in style.

🌿

Ecofacts

Naturally occurring biological or non-biological materials found in association with human activity. Seeds, pollen, animal bones, soils. Ecofacts reveal ancient diets, environments, farming practices, and climate — without humans ever touching them directly.

🪨

Features

Human-made traces not designed to be portable. Hearths, pits, postholes, ditches, Stonehenge. Features can only be recorded in place — removing them destroys them. They reveal organization, activity patterns, and permanent human modifications to the landscape.

🏛️

Structures

Human-built architecture with closed, defined space — walls and bounded interior. A complex type of feature. Structures range from simple shelters to the great monuments of antiquity, each telling stories of social organization and belief.

CONTEXT is the most important concept in archaeology

Where something is found, and what it is found alongside, are often more important than the object itself. Looting destroys context — and with it, most of what an artifact could tell us.

Matrix The physical substance surrounding the find — soil, sediment, deposit type
Provenience The exact 3D position within the site — recorded to the centimeter
Association The relationship to other finds in the same deposit — what was found together

Formation Processes: How the Record Forms

C-Transforms

Cultural formation processes — human activities that create, modify, or destroy the archaeological record. Deliberate burial (hoards, graves), construction, abandonment, reuse, warfare, burning. When a pot is buried intentionally vs. discarded accidentally, the context of that act is archaeologically readable.

N-Transforms

Natural formation processes — environmental events that alter buried material. Includes Floralturbation Faunalturbation Cryoturbation Argilliturbation Graviturbation. Also natural disasters like Vesuvius — which, paradoxically, perfectly preserved Pompeii through destruction.

Methods

Finding the Past

Excavation is only one part of the process — and a destructive one at that. The sequence from research design to publication spans years, with most archaeologists spending far more time on analysis than digging.

1

Research Design & Literary Clues

Formulate a strategy. Search historical records, maps, and previous reports. Literary clues narrow the search area before a single trowel is lifted.

2

Ground Survey & Remote Sensing

Non-destructive assessment of the site. Identify what's there before committing to excavation. Sampling strategies include systematic transects and boustrophadon patterns (back-and-forth, "like the ox").

3

Excavation

Destructive — can never be undone. Major ethical weight. Methods: Wheeler Box-Grid (balks for profiles), Open-Area excavation (best for single-period sites), Test Pits (exploratory). Context sheets record every layer in precise detail.

4

Post-Excavation Analysis & Publication

Most of an archaeologist's actual work. Artifact sorting, dating, environmental sampling, illustration, writing. The Harris Matrix organizes stratigraphy. Analysis turns raw data into historical knowledge that must be published for science to progress.

⚡ LEGO Excavation — In-Class Lab

We excavated miniature LEGO sites using authentic Umm Al-Jimal Archaeological Project context recording sheets — the same forms Prof. Rohl uses in Jordan. The exercise revealed how much information must be captured: matrix type, color, texture, inclusions, relationships to adjacent contexts. Every layer is a separate "context" with its own story. Stratigraphy shows change over time vertically; horizontal exposure shows activity at a single moment.

📐 Stratigraphy & the Harris Matrix

The Law of Superposition: lower layers are older. But formation processes complicate this. The Harris Matrix is a visual diagram that maps the sequence of deposits across a site, showing which contexts are above, below, or contemporary with others — even when they can't be physically adjacent.

Remote Sensing

Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR)

Emits radar pulses underground and maps reflections to produce 3D models of buried remains. Used at Forum Novum (Italy) to map an entire Roman street grid without excavation.

Remote Sensing

Magnetometry

Detects magnetic distortions caused by kilns, hearths, and pits. Fired clay and burning dramatically alter the local magnetic field — visible to a magnetometer from the surface.

Aerial Survey

LiDAR

Laser scanning that penetrates tree canopy to reveal digital elevation models of the ground below. Used at Caracol (Belize) to discover an entire hidden Maya city — changing estimates of ancient population size dramatically.

Spatial Analysis

GIS

Geographic Information Systems store, manage, and analyze spatial data in layers. Used for mapping sites, modeling predictive distributions, and integrating remote sensing data into navigable maps.

Surface Survey

Systematic Transects

Fieldwalkers move along predetermined lines across a landscape, recording what they find on the surface. Boustrophadon pattern (back-and-forth) ensures complete coverage. Sampling strategies balance coverage against resources.

Lab Work

Artifact Processing

Sorting, cleaning, labeling, drawing, and cataloguing finds. The Archaeology Lab (HH 494) includes data entry, artifact organization, and document preservation — 10 hours of hands-on engagement with real archaeological materials.

Chronology

Dating the Past

Without dates, archaeology produces a jumbled pile of things. Chronological frameworks — both relative and absolute — give the past its sequence and meaning.

Relative Dating

Stratigraphy

Lower = older. The foundational method. Complicated by formation processes (intrusions, inversions), but still the first tool applied at any excavation.

Typology

Objects change style over time in recognizable ways. A pottery assemblage from an unknown deposit can be dated by comparing its forms to known sequences from dated sites.

Seriation — "Battleship Curves"

Plot the frequency of artifact types over time — they rise, peak, then decline in a shape resembling a battleship viewed from above. Sorts assemblages into chronological sequence.

TPQ & TAQ

Terminus Post Quem: the date after which a deposit formed (e.g., a coin minted in 50 CE cannot be buried before 50 CE). Terminus Ante Quem: the date before which the deposit must predate.

Absolute Dating

Dendrochronology — Tree-Ring Dating

Annual rings in trees are counted and matched to known sequences. The most accurate dating method — annual precision. Limited to temperate zones and specific preserved wood. Also used to calibrate radiocarbon dates.

Radiocarbon (¹⁴C) Dating

Carbon-14 decays at a constant rate. Half-life = 5,730 years. Only dates organic materials (wood, bone, charcoal). Calibration is mandatory because atmospheric ¹⁴C concentration changes over time — calibration curves align radiocarbon years to calendar years.

Dating Systems: BC/AD vs BCE/CE vs BP

BC/AD = Christian calendar. BCE/CE = secular equivalent. BP = Before Present, with "Present" fixed at 1950 (when radiocarbon dating was standardized). Knowing which system a source uses is essential to avoid confusion by 2,000 years.

¹⁴C Decay — Percentage Remaining Over Half-Life Periods
Year 0 (death of organism)100%
5,730 years later (1 half-life)50%
11,460 years (2 half-lives)25%
17,190 years (3 half-lives)12.5%

After ~50,000 years, too little ¹⁴C remains for reliable measurement — the effective limit of radiocarbon dating.

Post-Midterm Coverage

Beyond the Dig Site

The second half of the semester expanded from methods to meaning — what the material record tells us about ancient environments, people's bodies, trade networks, belief systems, and our responsibilities as inheritors of the past.

🌿

Environmental Archaeology

Pollen analysis, seed assemblages, faunal remains, and soil chemistry reveal ancient climates and human-environment interactions. The interplay between sustainable land use and societal collapse — visible in the archaeological record across civilizations.

⚗️

Artifacts, Production & Trade

The Uluburun shipwreck (c. 1300 BCE, Turkey) demonstrates a Late Bronze Age globalized trade network — copper from Cyprus, tin from unknown sources, glass from Egypt, ivory from Africa, Mycenaean swords. Production chains visible in material culture. The iPhone lifecycle as an analogy: acquisition → manufacturing → use → deposition.

💀

Bioarchaeology

Reading the body as evidence: age, sex, health, diet, activity patterns, and cause of death from skeletal and soft tissue remains. Isotope analysis traces migration. DNA sequences reconstruct kinship and population movements. Every skeleton is a life story compressed into bone.

🧠

Cognitive Archaeology & Belief

How do we identify religion, ritual, and symbolic thought in the material record? Evidence of intentional burial, grave goods, rock art, and sacred spaces suggest cognitive complexity far earlier than once assumed. Archaeology reaches toward the inner life of past peoples.

⚖️

Ethics, Repatriation & CRM

Cultural Resource Management mandates protection of heritage during development — the developer pays. NAGPRA (Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act). The Elgin Marbles debate. Who owns the past? The material record is a public resource, and archaeologists are its stewards, not its owners.

🌍

Heritage & World Archaeology

UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The Umm Al-Jimal Archaeological Project (inscribed 2024) in Jordan — a case study in how academic archaeology, local communities, and international heritage frameworks intersect. Heritage as living relationship with the past, not just museum pieces.

Calvin University Context

Archaeology & Christian Faith

One of the most distinctive aspects of this course at Calvin — two full sessions devoted to the relationship between the archaeological record and Christian scripture, heritage, and interpretation.

The Tension: When Evidence Challenges Expectation

Kathleen Kenyon's excavations at Jericho — one of the most theologically charged sites in archaeology — pushed the dates of occupation back by several centuries compared to a literal reading of the biblical account. This created significant tension between faith communities hoping for archaeological confirmation and the evidence as Kenyon found it.

The lesson is not that archaeology disproves faith, but that evidence must be followed wherever it leads. Pseudo-archaeology, including faith-motivated cherry-picking of archaeological data, fails both intellectually and spiritually.

"A brave space is a space where grace and truth abound." — Prof. Rohl, course syllabus

Archaeology as Theological Resource

At the same time, archaeology has enormously enriched our understanding of the biblical world. The Dead Sea Scrolls. The Siloam Inscription. Umm Al-Jimal's early Christian community, built in basalt and inscription. The physical reality of ancient spaces deepens, not diminishes, the weight of historical religious claims.

Prof. Rohl's own work at Umm Al-Jimal — a UNESCO World Heritage Site on the Byzantine-Arab borderland — is itself an example of how archaeological inquiry and Christian heritage coexist. The site's churches, inscriptions in Greek and early Arabic, and evidence of a multicultural transition period are living testimony to the complexity of early Christian history.

Helena Augusta identified pilgrimage sites; Nabonidus restored ancient temples. The impulse to reach back and honor the past is itself ancient.
Personal Growth

What This Course Changed

Three shifts in how I think about the past, evidence, and the human story — produced by a semester of paying attention.

01

Context is Everything — Literally

Before this course, I thought archaeology was about finding impressive objects. Now I understand that an artifact without context is almost meaningless — a ripped-out page from a book. The LEGO excavation made this visceral: removing an object from its layer without recording the matrix, provenience, and associations destroys irreplaceable information. Looting isn't just theft. It's the deliberate erasure of history.

02

97% Is Unwritten

The single statistic that reoriented how I think about human history: for about 97% of the time our species has existed, there were no written records. Everything we know about that 97% comes from archaeology alone. Writing-centric history is a recent and culturally narrow lens. Archaeology is the discipline that gives voice to the vast majority of human experience — including peoples who have been deliberately silenced or marginalized.

03

Evidence Over Conclusion

Processual archaeology overreached. Pseudo-archaeology starts at the destination. The lesson running through every part of this course — from Kenyon at Jericho to the debate over Paleolithic scavenging vs. hunting — is that good reasoning follows the evidence wherever it leads, even when the conclusion is uncomfortable. That's not just a methodological principle. It's an ethical one.

⚗️ Arthur's O'on — A Roman Folly in Scotland

A small Roman domed structure — long demolished — that stood near the Antonine Wall in Scotland, within the area of Prof. Rohl's own earlier research. It appeared in our class discussion of processual and post-processual interpretation: the same physical remains can be read as a temple, a victory monument, or a surveying datum depending on interpretive framework. The building is gone; the argument about what it meant continues.
